Abstract 

High-Bandwidth Flash (HBF) stacks NAND behind a wide, package-local interface, giving flash-scale capacity with far better read la-tency and bandwidth than an SSD. This makes it tempting to keep an SSD-style Mooncake KV-offloading stack and swap only the backing tier for HBF. We test that substitution with an extended To-kenSim, four complete two-hour Qwen-Bailian production traces, five dense and mixture-of-experts models, and H100/B200 profiles. Serving gets worse, not better, and a cost-benefit model explains why. A faster far tier helps only when read I/O is the serving bot-tleneck, reads outweigh writes, and delivered bandwidth is sustain-able. All three must hold together, and transient KV fails every one. The package trade that buys flash costs GPU near-tier capacity and bandwidth, so average end-to-end latency rises 2–5 .5× and maxi-mum SLO goodput falls 1 .1–2 .7× across H100 and B200. Serving is almost insensitive to HBF’s own read/write latency, and base-die near-memory compute does not raise the flash tier’s share of the critical path. The two-tier hierarchy keeps reuse in the near tier and hands HBF a write-heavy stream, so writes outnumber reads on every trace. A 3D-ICE model shows that stream drives the stack to its thermal limit well below peak bandwidth, and a TLC tier wears out sooner than a capacity-matched SSD pool. The faster device yields a slower system because the package gives up more than the medium returns. HBF is not the problem; using it as a faster SSD for transient KV is. It belongs in serving as a selective, reuse-aware, write-budgeted, and thermally coordinated tier, not as a drop-in SSD replacement.

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) inference stores a key and value vec-tor for every retained token. The key–value (KV) cache grows with context length, batch size, and active requests, limiting concur-rency and retained context. Paged allocation, quantization, and se-lective retention reduce waste or bytes per token [7, 12, 15, 23] but not the capacity limit. When the working set of prefixes and his-tories exceeds GPU memory, the system must place KV in a larger tier. Modern serving systems offload reusable KV to a larger tier. Reuse comes from repeated dialogue, common prefixes in shared prompts and documents, and context revisited by agent workflows. Mooncake and related systems therefore extend the KV pool into CPU DRAM, remote memory, and SSDs [4, 16]. This offloading is standard and essential: without it, a capacity-limited server must reject requests or recompute evicted KV. It supplies the objects that larger tiers store and restore. High-Bandwidth Flash (HBF) aims to accelerate that tier. Roadmap HBF stacks many NAND dies for internal parallelism and uses the accelerator memory path instead of a PCIe/NVMe link [9]. Com-pared with an SSD, HBF has far lower read latency and far higher bandwidth. It can help when read-mostly objects and system dataflow are co-designed for HBF [6, 8]. A simple design keeps the Moon-cake object path, replaces its SSD backing tier with HBF, and ex-pects faster serving. Whether that simple design works is untested. The HBF studies above choose what to place in flash, so they do not say what hap-pens when a running KV hierarchy hands the device whatever it evicts. Device microbenchmarks do not settle it either, because the swap changes more than access time. The package that gains flash gives up near memory, and the traffic the tier receives is decided by the runtime rather than by the medium, so the answer depends on admission and queueing behavior that only a full serving stack exposes. This paper tests that design and finds it does not work. Under the same runtime, we compare complete HBF-1 and HBF-2 organi-zations against SSD baselines using TokenSim, four complete two-hour production traces, five dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) models, and H100/B200 profiles. HBF is slower on every request metric: across H100 and B200, average end-to-end latency rises 2– 5.5×, and maximum service-level objective (SLO) goodput falls 1 .1– 2.7×. Faster flash barely helps; changing HBF read/write latency by 3.75 × moves average end-to-end latency by less than 1%. The problem is the SSD-style hierarchy, not HBF itself. It sends HBF its least friendly object, transient and write-heavy KV, whose traffic collides with every physical limit of NAND. The boundary is simple: faster media helps only when secondary-tier service is on the request critical path (C1), later reads repay each flash write (C2), and delivered bandwidth is sustainable (C3). § 5 formalizes these as three necessary conditions, each with a falsifiable requirement

C1—Read I/O is the serving bottleneck. Complete HBF-1 and HBF-2 gain flash capacity by reducing GPU near-tier capacity and bandwidth, so the near tier admits fewer requests while the flash service HBF accelerates is a small fraction of the critical path (Find-ing 1, § 6.1). Base-die attention compute cannot raise that fraction (Finding 2, § 6.2). 

C2—Reads outweigh writes. The two-tier hierarchy serves reuse from the near tier and sends HBF a write-heavy cold stream, so writes outnumber reads on every trace (Finding 3, § 7.1). The SSD write-batching optimization that might lower their cost does not transfer (Finding 4, § 7.2). 

C3—Read/write Performance sustains. That stream drives the stack to its thermal limit well below peak bandwidth, forcing plane throttling (Finding 5, § 8.1). Without an SSD controller, the tier also wears out sooner than the SSD pool it replaces (Finding 6, § 8.2). The result does not argue against HBF or offloading, and this paper makes four contributions: (1) A model of when a faster far-memory tier helps LLM serv-ing, with three necessary conditions: read-I/O exposure, reads per write, and sustained bandwidth. It separates HBF media speed from the accelerator-local capacity, bandwidth, and queueing changes of a complete HBF organization. (2) Measurements showing why an SSD-style hierarchy sends HBF a write-heavy, low-reuse stream, using per-tier byte counters over four production traces. (3) A 3D-ICE thermal model and a write-endurance budget that turn HBF’s power and wear pressures into sourced, clearly bounded estimates. (4) Requirements for a critical-path-aware, reuse-aware, write-budgeted, and thermally managed HBF KV tier. 

2 Background 

This section explains how inference creates, reuses, and offloads KV state and why serving systems increasingly move it to a larger tier. Section 3 introduces HBF separately to distinguish storage pol-icy from device. 

2.1 KV Cache and Reuse in Agentic Serving 

Agentic and conversational workloads dominate modern LLM de-ployments: multi-turn dialogue, tool-use loops, shared system prompts, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). They revisit tokens, reusing prompt prefixes and session histories across requests. Serv-ing therefore depends less on raw compute than on memory capac-ity to retain useful prefixes and active histories and bandwidth to restore or stream them without stalling the GPU. LLM inference has prefill and decode phases. Prefill processes prompt tokens in parallel and materializes KV state. A prefix-cache hit avoids recomputing the matched prompt but still requires lo-cating and delivering its saved blocks to the GPU. Decode gener-ates tokens autoregressively and repeatedly reads the retained KV history. Each transformer layer stores a key and value vector per token. For 𝑁 retained tokens, the unsharded per-request KV foot-print is 

𝐵 KV /request = 2𝑁 𝐿𝐻 KV 𝐷 head 𝑏, (1) Here, 𝐿 , 𝐻 KV , 𝐷 head , and 𝑏 denote layer count, KV-head count, head dimension, and bytes per element; the factor of two accounts for keys and values. Grouped-query and latent-attention designs re-duce this footprint but do not remove the capacity problem. Unlike static shared weights, KV is generated online, grows dur-ing decode, and has request-, session-, and prefix-specific lifetimes. Stored prefixes matter only if later requests reuse them before evic-tion. Continuous batching adds pressure: admissions consume KV capacity, long requests retain blocks longer, and restoring offloaded state can delay execution. KV placement, reuse, and request sched-uling must therefore be optimized together. 

2.2 Paged and Pooled KV Storage 

2.2.1 PagedAttention. vLLM introduces PagedAttention and block-level KV allocation [12]. A block table maps each request’s log-ical KV blocks to non-contiguous physical blocks in GPU mem-ory. On-demand allocation reduces fragmentation, supports con-tinuous growth, and enables common-prefix sharing and copy-on-write without a large contiguous reservation. Two requests share exact-prefix KV when their token prefixes and model state match. The benefit grows with matched length but vanishes after eviction. System prompts and multi-turn dialogue create heavy reuse; independent API calls create little. The same admission policy can therefore yield very different read/write be-havior across traces. 

2.2.2 Mooncake and distributed KV pools. Paged allocation solves on-GPU layout but not capacity beyond HBM. Mooncake extends KV into a distributed, KV-cache-centric pool of CPU DRAM, SSDs, and network resources. It combines a transfer engine with KV-aware, SLO-aware scheduling that rejects work early under over-load [16]. KV-cache-centric scheduling treats KV-block location, avail-ability, reuse, and transfer cost as first-class state beyond request selection. SSD offloading is asynchronous and outside the write path. New KV lands in DRAM; after DRAM crosses a watermark, a background policy flushes or evicts it to SSD. A master node tracks per-object locations and promotes hot blocks back [11]. Flash writes are there-fore scheduling decisions, separate from GPU compute. Save and load move KV in opposite directions: save: GPU HBM → cache tier , (2) load: cache tier → GPU HBM , (3) Their imbalance is central to the write/read behavior quantified in Section 7.1. 

2.3 SSD Offloading of KV 

2.3.1 Why SSD offloading helps. HBM and host DRAM are fast but capacity-limited. SSDs add larger, cheaper, replaceable capacity and retain prefixes over the longer reuse windows needed by long-context, multi-turn, RAG, and agent workflows. DeepSeek’s API documents a production disk-based context cache with persisted prefix units and cache-hit/miss token accounting [2].

Production traces show bimodal reuse: most occurs within min-utes, but a long tail returns only after tens of minutes. Recency-only eviction therefore discards blocks that would still be reused [11]. SSD offloading is now standard and provides KV objects for larger tiers to store and restore. 

2.3.2 Modern offload paths. In a naive SSD path, data moves through host memory (GPU HBM → CPU I/O → NVMe → SSD). Submis-sion overhead, extra copies, and fragmented small requests can then dominate media service (Fig. 1(a)). Modern paths remove much of this cost (Fig. 1(b)). Linux io_uring 

uses shared queues to reduce system calls and context switches [13]. GPU Direct Storage (GDS) enables direct DMA between NVMe and GPU memory. Tutti moves object identification and completion to a GPU-side io_uring path, removing the CPU from each opera-tion’s critical path [17]. SSD offloading is therefore a strong, actively optimized base-line. Drives remain replaceable outside the accelerator package, and their controllers provide buffering, mapping, garbage collec-tion, wear leveling, and bad-block management. SSD remains off-package and bounded by the PCIe/NVMe link. This motivates High-Bandwidth Flash (HBF), a higher-bandwidth, package-local alternative introduced in Section 3. We evaluate HBF by replacing or augmenting the SSD backing tier while preserving the KV-object path above. 

3 High-Bandwidth Flash 

We describe the physical HBF device separately from the software path. The published roadmap supplies organization and generation parameters [9]; HAVEN’s modeled NAND arrays supply device la-tency, energy, bandwidth, and package power [8]. 

3.1 HBF Organization and Stacking 

HBF stacks 3D NAND dies behind wide vertical or package-level in-terconnects. It also includes a base die or distributed peripheral cir-cuits, many independently accessible NAND regions, and a package-level link to the GPU. Together, these components provide much more internal parallelism and interface bandwidth than a conven-tional SSD. The key distinction is simple: HBF changes how NAND connects to the processor; it does not change NAND into DRAM. Base Die

Reads sense pages or subarrays. Programs are slower than reads, and erases use larger blocks. NAND lacks arbitrary in-place over-writes, so invalid pages require later reclamation. Aggregate band-width comes from concurrency across channels, dies, planes, and subarrays, not DRAM-like latency for each access. A representative stack integrates sixteen 128–256-layer NAND dies, through-silicon-via (TSV) arrays, and a base die. The base die concentrates mapping and management logic near the package, a natural control-logic location that also concentrates area and heat (Fig. 2) [9]. HBF connects through the GPU memory path rather than as an NVMe block device. The GPU controller identifies HBF addresses, and the base die translates requests into NAND operations. This can avoid host software and PCIe/NVMe traversal but does not guarantee DRAM-like latency or write behavior. A read selects the HBF address, channel, die, plane, block, and page. It then senses NAND and returns data through TSVs and the package interface. The system places the object in HBM or a GPU-visible buffer before the request executes. A write allocates a phys-ical page, programs NAND, updates logical-to-physical metadata, invalidates any older page, and eventually reclaims invalid space. Wear, bad-block, error-correction, and thermal metadata must re-main consistent across this lifecycle.

Without this separation, media service, local memory, interconnect, and package power are insep-arable (Section 4). 

3.3 Advantages of HBF 

3.3.1 Capacity and bandwidth. NAND provides much greater den-sity than DRAM. A 512-GB HBF device paired with a 24-GB HBM stack yields a density ratio of ≈ 21 .3. HBF replaces the narrow PCIe/NVMe path with a wide package interface and exposes many NAND regions in parallel. The inter-face is only one bound on effective application bandwidth: 

𝐵𝑊 effective = min (𝐵𝑊 interface , 𝐵𝑊 array , 𝐵𝑊 controller ,𝐵𝑊 power , 𝐵𝑊 thermal , 𝐵𝑊 workload ). (5) Peak bandwidth therefore requires enough independent reads, con-troller concurrency, and power headroom. 

3.3.2 Shorter path and a cooperative hierarchy. HBF removes PCIe/N-VMe command processing, host staging, and board links. Modern GDS- and Tutti-style SSD paths narrow this advantage, so we use a strong SSD baseline. In the intended cooperative hierarchy, HBM holds weights, active KV, and write-heavy state; HBF holds larger read-mostly reusable objects; and SSD keeps colder capacity. A NAND read remains slow, but die-, plane-, and channel-level par-allelism provides high aggregate throughput when the runtime ex-poses concurrency. 

3.4 Limitations of HBF 

The wide interface does not change NAND, so HBF inherits three device-level limits relative to HBM. First, latency : NAND reads and writes take on the order of microseconds, roughly two orders of magnitude longer than HBM accesses; programs are slower and more energy-intensive than reads [8]. Die-, plane-, and channel-level parallelism raises aggregate bandwidth but does not close this per-access gap. Second, endurance : flash cells tolerate finite program/erase cy-cles, and elevated temperature accelerates wear [1]. A write-heavy tier therefore degrades far faster than DRAM’s effectively unbounded write endurance. Third, power and thermals : per-stack HBF power is far higher than HBM. H3 assumes roughly 160 W per HBF cube, so six stacks can approach 960 W [6], potentially exceeding the GPU die’s peak power. Package-local HBF therefore creates a new power and ther-mal problem rather than inheriting HBM’s modest envelope.

4.2 Workloads, Models, and Platforms 

We use four anonymized production-derived traces from the Aliyun Qwen-Bailian deployment [19]. Each contains a complete two-hour window with fixed request order, lengths, session structure, and 16-token KV identifiers. traceA mixes production traffic with conver-sational reuse. traceB contains short-output API/text traffic with-out multi-turn sessions, although shared prefixes still create cross-request reuse. coder contains code-oriented multi-turn context, and thinking combines long reasoning outputs with a smaller multi-turn population. A reused block is a unique hash identifier that appears in more than one request. We evaluate five dense and MoE models. Table 2(b) summarizes the architectural parameters used by the current TokenSim profiles. For MoE models, the parameter count reports both total parame-ters and parameters activated per token. We model eight-GPU H100 and B200 systems with tensor paral-lelism 8, pipeline and data parallelism 1, and expert parallelism for MoE models. The SSD tier uses 3.2-TB KIOXIA CM7-V drives with 14.0/6.75 GB/s sequential read/write bandwidth and 3 TB modeled usable capacity per drive [10]. The main SSD baseline is the con-ventional Mooncake connector in the simulator. We evaluate offered loads from 0.05 to 32 QPS by rescaling request-arrival timestamps while preserving request order, contents, ses-sions, and KV-reuse relationships. 

4.3 Metrics and Statistical Treatment 

We report TTFT, TBT, end-to-end latency, delivered request and to-ken throughput, queueing and connector delay, KV hits, blocks and bytes read and written, the write/read ratio, prefix reuse, recompu-tation, and SLO goodput. For the architecture comparison we aver-age each metric over the trace and model campaign at every system and latency point, because the question is how the complete SSD, HBF-1, and HBF-2 organizations behave, not how one favorable point behaves. The write/read ratio and prefix hit rate in Finding 3 come from the per-tier byte counters over a full two-hour replay of each trace, so they reflect fixed request contents and ordering rather than a sampled window. Zero-read runs are excluded from the ratio because it is undefined, and malformed result files are ex-cluded. Throughout, we label every value as a measured simulator output, a sourced device parameter, or a modeled projection. 

4.4 Thermal Model 

Two findings need device models the serving simulator does not provide. The thermal model is described here, and the write-endurance budget is given with Finding 6 (§8.2). For thermal behavior we build a 16-Hi HBF stack similar to HBM4, with 128-layer 3D-NAND TLC dies, and solve its steady-state heat flow with 3D-ICE [18]. We drive the model with the read and write energy of a 16-token KV block—the connector’s access granularity—and sweep sustained single-stack bandwidth to obtain dynamic power and peak tem-perature. We set the safe junction temperature to 80 ◦C, match-ing HBM3e, and model a throttling policy that closes the hottest, highest-traffic planes and redirects writes to cooler planes once the limit is reached. The thermal model is a projection for unreleased hardware; we report it as such and use it to bound, not fix, the thermal limits of an HBF tier.

6.2 Finding 2: Base-Die Compute Cannot Raise the Exposed Fraction 

Finding 1 leaves 𝑓 near 1%. One reading of that number is that the compute sits too far from the data, in which case moving the compute to the data should raise 𝑓 . The standard form of that move is a near-memory attention engine on the HBF base die, as HBM-PIM does for DRAM. We ask whether it does. We model a decode-attention engine on the base die under as-sumptions chosen to favor HBF. It is charged no area, power, or pro-grammability penalty, and every eligible token hits at the device service time, so a negative result is a lower bound. The two modes differ in reach. The HBM engine accelerates all HBM-resident KV; the HBF engine reaches only KV already resident in HBF, and hot HBM-resident KV falls back to GPU attention. Table 5 lists the full configuration. Fig. 8(a) reports both engines. Averaged over the 40 sweep points, the HBM engine cuts decode-attention time by 42 .84%, and it is sta-ble across the whole sweep (42 .78–42 .86%). The HBF engine aver-ages −0.03% on the same basis, that is, no benefit at all. Individual points swing from −29 .05% to +20 .49%, and it is slower than GPU attention at 16 of 40 points, turning positive only near saturation. The end-to-end effect is smaller again. Replacing the HBF engine with the much stronger HBM engine moves decode p50 by 0 .39% and decode p99 by 0 .82% on non-saturated points. Two quantities account for this behavior. The first is coverage .A base-die engine sees only the cold KV the runtime has already placed in HBF, and the mean HBF-resident fraction is just 15 .48% (Fig. 8(b)), so even a perfect HBF engine cannot touch the common HBM-resident case. The conclusion is not that base-die com-pute is useless but that it is the wrong lever for this bottleneck. 7.2 Finding 4: SSD Write-Batching helps little for HBF 

Finding 3 leaves 𝜌 fixed by object class, so the remaining lever in Eq. (8) is the write cost 𝐶 . SSD KV serving already has the stan-dard tool for lowering it. Mooncake-style batching coalesces many small KV writes into large buckets, amortizing the drive’s slow per-operation I/O and turning scattered writes into near-sequential transfers. Whether that win is a property of the optimization or of the medium it was built for decides whether it carries onto HBF. We run the identical bucketing mechanism on both media over a shared traceA /traceB matrix, holding HBF’s random and se-quential bandwidth equal so batching can help only by amortizing fixed write-operation latency. We compare three arms, no-batch, the Mooncake bucketed path, and a single-key control that pays the bucketing cost without grouping anything. Table 8 and Fig. 9(a) give the paired result. On SSD-12, request p50 falls by 8 .9–21 .1% at every block size. The same mechanism on HBF-2 recovers 2 .69% overall, 5 .2× smaller, and the benefit is concentrated at the smallest block, +9.2% at 16 tokens, fading to 

+0.72% at 128 and turning negative (−1.85%) at 512. The SSD gain is flat across block size; the HBF gain decays to zero and then costs. 

Composition of the write cost. The contrast follows from what each medium’s write cost is made of. On SSD a write is dominated by high fixed per-operation overhead and a random-access penalty, and usable bandwidth is low relative to demand. Batching attacks all three at once, so its win is large and survives coarse blocks. HBF removes those levers by construction. Its access latency is low and its random and sequential bandwidth are equal, so there is no scattered-to-sequential conversion and no bandwidth cliff. The only lever left is amortizing fixed operation latency, which pays off only when a bucket is dense with tiny blocks, about 94 blocks per bucket at 16 tokens but 4 at 512 (Fig. 9(b)). The single-key control makes that residue explicit. With the batching path enabled but no grouping it is worse than no-batch on every metric, because it pays bucketing overhead for no amortization. Batching helps SSD because it compensates for SSD’s specific weaknesses. HBF does not have those weaknesses, so the optimiza-tion has almost nothing left to lower. The write cost 𝐶 cannot be engineered down, so C2 still fails. 

Finding 4 SSD-style write batching does not transfer to HBF be-cause the medium lacks the latency and access-pattern penalties that batching amortizes.

Why the stack runs hot. Delivered bandwidth is not a constant. It varies in time as hot regions throttle and cool, and in space as the runtime steers traffic away from the hottest planes. The cause traces back to Finding 3. Because reuse stays in the near tier and writes dominate the flash stream at higher power per access, the device is pushed to its limit by exactly the write-heavy traffic the hierarchy sends it. A read-mostly workload at the same bandwidth would run cooler. A scheduler that assumes a fixed flash bandwidth will mispre-dict service time and build queues it did not expect. Stable, reusable objects fit the device; transient KV does not.

Every write spends part of a fi-nite program/erase budget, so whether the device survives its write volume over a deployment life is the second half of sustained de-livery, and it is open.

We measure the write volume the traces generate and compare it against two endurance envelopes. The HBF write counter records the media-write volume each trace sends to an eight-H100 HBF-2 tier over the two-hour replay, normalized to a daily rate. The SSD reference is a capacity-matched pool of four KIOXIA CM7-V 3.2 TB drives [10], whose vendor 3-DWPD rating gives 38 .4 TB/day. For HBF we adopt a labeled, deliberately favorable TLC assumption with unit write amplification, which yields a 21 .7 TB/day budget for a five-year target; any real NAND management would only lower it. Every trace writes past both envelopes (48–140 TB/day), so un-der linear TBW exhaustion the nominal HBF tier lasts a constant 0.56 × the SSD pool’s life across all four traces (Fig. 11). Coarsening KV blocks from 16 to 128 to 512 tokens raises the apparent prefix hit rate yet increases HBF write volume from 188 to 237 to 258 TB (1 .38 ×). 

Why the shortfall is structural. HBF is attached like memory but behaves like flash. An SSD hides the cost of writing NAND behind a controller, and an HBF stack, presented to the GPU as memory, has no equivalent place to put one. The services that con-troller provides for free (logical-to-physical mapping, write buffer-ing, garbage collection, TRIM, wear leveling, bad-block handling, and error correction) must be rebuilt in the base die, GPU mem-ory controller, driver, or runtime [3]. That rebuilt control plane is the movement and control term 𝐶 move of Eq. (6), work the SSD tier never charged to the serving system. Managing the write side needs device state (write budget, wear, hot regions), not a single peak-bandwidth number. Block granularity does not route around that state. Small blocks maximize the number of write operations, while large blocks maximize bytes written and write amplification, because partial-block reuse forces whole larger blocks to be rewrit-ten. Both keep the device on the wrong side of Eq. (8). HBF therefore wears out sooner than the SSD it replaces, which is the endurance term 𝐶 life of Eq. (6), now measured. A design writing short-lived KV into HBF pays twice, once for the slow unbuffered program and again against a write budget it exhausts faster.

9 Discussion and Related Work 9.1 Synthesis 

The six findings falsify the three conditions of Section 5 in turn, and together they mark where HBF does help. An object belongs in HBF only when its restore delay is the serving bottleneck (C1), its reads outweigh its writes ( 𝜌 > 𝜌 ★, C2), and the bandwidth it needs is sustainable under the package’s power and endurance budget (C3). Fig. 12 places representative object classes on the two axes that decide this (exposed stall and reads-per-write), with sustain-able bandwidth shifting the boundary. Transient decode KV sits in the low-exposure, low-𝜌 corner where all three conditions fail; weights and shared prefixes sit in the opposite corner, which is where H3 and HAVEN place their HBF-resident state. The three conditions use device-agnostic quantities: exposed critical-path fraction, reads per write, and sustained bandwidth. The same map therefore applies to other package-local far tiers such as CXL-attached or near-data flash, not only to HBF. The map gives the axes and the break-even rule of Eq. (8) rather than a calibrated boundary across the latency ×bandwidth plane. A work-ing runtime requires flat GPU-managed access, reuse-aware admis-sion, and thermal-aware write budgeting. This paper’s role is to establish, with measurements, why that design is needed. 

9.2 Related Work 

KV-cache management. PagedAttention enables block allocation and prefix sharing [12], quantization and compaction shrink KV size [7, 15, 23], and Prompt Cache and CachedAttention reuse state across prompts [4, 5]. Mooncake, LMCache, Strata, and Tutti of-fload KV across CPU, SSD, and network tiers with GPU-assisted I/O [14, 16, 17, 22], and DeepSeek’s disk context cache evidences persisted prefix reuse [2]. We ask how this SSD-offload stack be-haves when its backing medium moves into the accelerator pack-age.

HBF. H3 pairs HBM with HBF for cost-efficient inference, storing read-only weights and shared precomputed KV in HBF while keep-ing generated state in HBM [6]. It demonstrates a favorable object split but does not model the write, thermal, or endurance behav-ior of transient KV. HAVEN keeps a read-mostly vector collection in HBF and reranks near the device [8]. FlashAccel goes furthest, integrating six HBF stacks into an HBM-based GPU and report-ing 2 .54 × throughput over an HBM-only GPU [20]. However, its gains rest on committing the entire tier to SLC flash, whose den-sity is about one-fourth of the TLC we assume, so the ∼21 × density edge over HBM narrows sharply. This is exactly the endurance-versus-density trade our reuse-aware, write-budgeted placement (Findings 3 and 6) navigates rather than pays across the whole tier. 

Flash reliability and evaluation. FTL out-of-place mapping, garbage collection, and wear management are mature in SSD controllers but must be reassigned when flash becomes a GPU-visible tier [3], and NAND retention depends on wear and temperature [1]. 

9.3 Limitations 

HBF is not a public product, so we label roadmap organization as sourced and device values as modeled or swept, and report cost as a qualitative gap rather than a calibrated ratio. Most importantly, the architecture comparison changes GPU-local memory together with the secondary tier, so it shows that the complete HBF orga-nizations underperform under this SSD-style runtime; it does not show that SSD is the better isolated medium. Simulator validation reduces risk but does not replace measurements on future HBF sil-icon, whose package routing, controller behavior, and long-term wear remain outside the present model. 

10 Conclusion 

Replacing an SSD KV tier with HBF does not improve LLM serving, under the same Mooncake-style runtime it makes serving worse. Our model explains why: a faster far tier helps only when read I/O is the serving bottleneck (C1), reads outweigh writes (C2), and its bandwidth is sustainable (C3), and all three fail for transient KV behind an SSD-style connector. HBF is not the problem, using it as a faster SSD for transient KV is.
